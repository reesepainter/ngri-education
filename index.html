Myths vs Facts

Myth:

Defendants who are found not guilty by reason of Insanity receive less time and they get let off scot-free.


VS.


Fact:

The institutional confinement of persons found NGRI is a common practice (Jones v. United States, 1983). Many individuals are held for extended periods of time, presumably for treatment purposes as well as protection of the public. As we noted earlier in this article, the standards for committing NGRI acquittees are quite different from the civil commitment standards. Unlike civil commitment detainees, NGRI acquittees are not committed solely for the purpose of treatment, and their release does not necessarily depend on their response to treatment. As Finkel (1988) pointed out, the expectations of both the legal system and the public have perhaps greater influence on both commitment and release decisions. In other words, notions of retribution and deterrence are likely to carry more weight than response to treatment. (Ogloff,1993)

Myth:

NGRI is commonly used throughout the legal system and can often be seen throughout many cases.


VS.


Fact:

Overall, the insanity defense was raised in one percent of all felony cases. Further, only 26 percent of those raising the insanity defense were actually acquitted NGRI. (Callahan et al., 1991)

Myth:

Defendants claiming to be mentally ill and NGRI are faking it, or are receiving an easy way out.


VS.


Fact:

Our data clearly illustrate that the vast majority of people who used the insanity defense were seriously mentally ill. Only 10 percent of the population raising the defense did not receive a DSM-111 diagnosis, and the large majority had a prior hospitalization. Furthermore, only the most disturbed defendants were successful in their plea. The popular concept that the insanity defense is an "easy out" for defendants who are either feigning mental illness or who claim temporary insanity is clearly untrue. (Callahan et al., 1991)

Legal Defintion

Not guilty by reason of insanity is a plea or affirmative defense by excuse entered by a defendant in a criminal trial, in which the defendant claims that they were so mentally disturbed or incapacitated at the time of the offense that they did not have the required intention or mens rea to commit the crime, and are therefore not guilty. Not guilty by reason of insanity can also be a verdict entered by a jury in a criminal case, stating that the defendant cannot be liable because of the defendant’s insanity. Such a verdict may require the defendant to be admitted into a mental institution. To prove legal insanity, defendants usually put forth expert testimony on their psychological evaluation. Various jurisdictions use various tests to judge such pleas (for example: M’Naghten Rule, cognitive test, irresistible impulse test, substantial capacity test, etc.). (Cornell Law)

Simplified Defintion

This is a defense that defendants can use in the court of law to help prove they are not guilty of a crime based on a mental disorder/disease that can be upheld by certain professions(pyschologists,etc). Legal standards or tests vary on what they qualify and look for in these cases, from a cognitive (right from wrong) , to even any correlation of mental illness at all, to not being able to control your actions.
