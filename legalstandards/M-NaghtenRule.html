Origin:

The House of Lords joined the uproar and requested the 15 judges of the common law courts to respond to a series of questions about the insanity defense. Lord Chief Justice Tindall, who had presided at M'Naghten's trial, responded for all but one of the justices. One segment of his response was adopted by the House of Lords as the formal test for insanity and has come to be known as the M'Naghten rules: A person is presumed sane unless it can be clearly proven that, at the time of the committing of the act, the party accused was labouring under such a defect of reason, from disease of the mind, as not to know the nature and quality of the act he was doing; or, if he did know it, that he did not know he was doing what was wrong. (Regina v. M'Naghten, 1843, p. 722) (Ogloff et al., 1993) Plus the origin of the standard in the US: Within 10 years of Justice Tindall's report, the federal and most of the state courts in the United States had adopted the M'Naghten rules as the standard test formulation of the insanity defense(Ogloff et al., 1993)

Modern Use:

Most standard of the legal standards and is frequently used throughout 20 + states and is the federal guidline for not guilty by reason of insanity.

Main Purpose/Focal Point:

The M'Naghten standard comprises three substantive elements. First, it must be determined that the defendant was suffering from "a defect of reason, from disease of the mind." Next, evidence must show that, as a result of the mental disease or defect, the defendant did not "know" the "nature and quality of the act he was doing." Finally, the M'Naghten standard also requires an inquiry to determine whether the defendant knew "what he was doing was wrong." Therefore, the defendant who has a cognitive understanding or "knowledge" of his or her act, yet who does not demonstrate knowledge that the act was morally wrong, may also be acquitted under the M'Naghten test. The M'Naghten test is referred to as a "cognitive" test of insanity because of its focus on the quality of the defendant's thought processes at the time of the crime (e.g., Low, Jeffries, & Bonnie, 1986). (Ogloff et al., 1993)

The M'Naghten standard or rule's main focus point is on if a defender knows from right and wrong to help prove if they are guilty or not of a crime (by reason of insanity). Think of it as a cognitived focused test for insanity.
