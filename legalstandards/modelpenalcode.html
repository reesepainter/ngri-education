The American Law Institute Test of Insanity With the exception of the Durham experiment, acceptance of the M'Naghten and volitional control tests was not challenged seriously until the ALI published its Model Penal Code in 1962. In drafting the new insanity defense standard, the ALI subcommittee attempted to redress the shortcomings of earlier formulations, especially the requirement of total incapacity, which seemed implicit in the M'Naghten and irresistible impulse tests. The resulting ALI standard, as set out in Section 4.01 of the Model Penal Code, is as follows: (1) A person is not responsible for criminal conduct if at the time of such conduct as a result of mental disease or defect he lacks substantial capacity either to appreciate the criminality (wrongfulness) of his conduct or to conform his conduct to the requirements of law. (2) As used in this Article, the terms "mental disease of defect" do not include an abnormality manifested only by repeated criminal or otherwise anti-social conduct. (ALI, 1962, §4.01) (Ogloff et al., 1993)

Modern Use:

Following the 1981 trial of John Hinckley Jr., the federal government and many states retreated from the broader MPC standard. The federal system and multiple states abandoned tests for the inability to control one's actions and returned to a stricter variant of the cognitive-only M'Naghten rule. Roughly half of the states use the Model Penal code or some variations of it.

Main Focus/Focal Point:

The main focus of the MPC test for NGRI cases is to combine both cognitive understand (a more well defined M'Naghten's Rule with the inability to control one's behavior (but not a complete lack of free will like the Irresistible Impulse Test) with a "substantial capacity" standard.
