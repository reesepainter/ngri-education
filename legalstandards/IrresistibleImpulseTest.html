Origin:

Criticism of the M'Naghten rules soon arose from medical and legal arenas, with most critics attacking the rules for their narrow focus on the defendant's cognitive knowledge. In response, an additional test which focused on the defendant's volitional controls soon came to supplement M'Naghten in many jurisdictions. One of the earliest expressions of the supplementary volitional control tests, or the irresistible impulse rules, as they are commonly named, was expressed in Parsons v. State (1886): [The defendant is not] legally responsible if the two following conditions concur: 1) If, by reason of the duress of... mental disease he had so far lost the power to choose between right and wrong, and to avoid doing the act in question, as that his free agency was at the time destroyed; 2) and if, at the same time, the alleged crime was so connected with such mental disease, in the relation of cause and effect, as to have been the product of it solely. (cited in Melton, Petrila, Poythress, & Slobogin, 1987, p. 116) (Ogloff et al., 1993)

Modern Use:

The federal Insanity Defense Reform Act of 1984 removed volitional impairment tests (like the irresistible impulse test) from federal courts but some states like Texas, New Mexico, Colorado, and Virginia pair it alongside the M'Naghten's Rule.

Main Focus/Focal Point

The main focus of the irresistible impulse test is on a defendant's lack of self-control due to a mental illness, rather than just their intellectual understanding of right and wrong as seen in the M'Naghten's Rule.
